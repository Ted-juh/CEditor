The browser knows what it sounds like.

VIP's preset system indexed files: it read what the vendor wrote on the tin, put it in four tag columns, and left you to click through 12,000 rows hoping one of them was the pad you could hear in your head. This indexes sounds — every one of them auditioned once, measured, drawn, and filed next to your hardware patches and your own chains, because a Juno patch and a .vstpreset are the same thing to a person looking for a bass.

The complaint, stated fairly

VIP's browser was the best in the business, and it was still a filing cabinet.

Everything below is aimed at one weakness, and it is worth naming precisely because "make it better" is not actionable: VIP knew a preset's name, its vendor, and whatever four tags a marketing intern typed. It never once listened to it.

Every consequence follows from that. Tags disagree between vendors because they were typed by different people. Auditioning is gated on plug-in load time, because there is nothing to play until the plug-in is up. "Init" appears forty times and nothing notices. A multi dies when a plug-in is uninstalled, because a name is all the multi ever knew about what it wanted. And a hardware synth — the thing a controller keyboard was sold to play — is not in the browser at all.

Plate 5 / substitutes

The match percentage is distance in the same descriptor space the Atlas plots, so it is explainable rather than magical: the chips under each candidate are the axes that agreed and the one that did not. This is the feature that only works because the library listened to everything once.

The mechanism

One probe, played once, and everything above falls out of it

The whole proposal rests on a single new job: play every sound once, offline, and keep what came back. It is not a research project — it is the plug-in scanner doing a second kind of work.

What the probe is

Instantiate the plug-in once, then walk its presets, loading each state and rendering offline: C3 at velocity 100 held 1.2 s, released, tail captured to 0.8 s; a second pass at velocity 40 for how it answers touch; a three-note chord for width. From that come the descriptors — spectral centroid and its drift, attack and release time, harmonic-to-noise ratio, stereo width, dynamic response, peak and RMS, pitch tracking, whether it made a sound at all — plus the CPU it took and the latency it reported.

Cost, stated honestly: the render is 50–200× real time; the expensive part is the plug-in accepting a state, which is 100–400 ms for a big instrument. Twelve thousand presets is therefore about an hour, out of process, resumable, incremental, and never repeated for a fingerprint it has already seen. VIP's scan was not faster and produced less.

What it keeps, and what that costs on disk

Forty-odd floats per record, which is nothing, and a 2-second mono snapshot at 16 kHz in FLAC — around 35 kB, roughly 430 MB across a twelve-thousand-preset library. That is real, so the snapshot is a cache and says so: descriptors and thumbprints are kept forever, snapshots are evicted least-recently-heard down to a budget you set, and a missing snapshot degrades to exactly today's behaviour — load the plug-in and wait.

The hole VIP never filled, and the way round it

Some instruments keep their presets to themselves. Omnisphere, Serum and Diva have their own browsers and expose little or nothing to a host, and this is precisely where VIP's promise of "every preset in one place" quietly stopped being true. Scanning cannot fix that, and pretending otherwise is how you get a browser that lies.

So don't scan them — listen while you work. When you browse inside a plug-in's own UI, the host already has the plug-in up and is already rendering its audio. Notice the state blob change, run the same descriptor pass over the audio that just came out, and offer a single unobtrusive "file this" with the name read from the plug-in where it exposes one. Browse Omnisphere for ten minutes and the library has learned ten minutes of Omnisphere, measured the same way as everything else, with your provenance on it. Nothing is filed without you saying so.

Order of work

Six stages, each of which ships alone

Stage A

The workspace

The slide-out list becomes a tab: rail, facets, grid, inspector, audition bar. Facets and exclusion over searchLibrary. Smart collections as stored queries. No analysis anywhere — this is a better browser over exactly today's data.

Front-end weighted · no new native subsystem
Stage B

The auditioner

The scanner worker learns to render a probe. Descriptors on the record, thumbprints in the grid, measured range filters, duplicate folding by fingerprint plus sonic distance. The expensive stage, and the one everything else needs.

Native · out of process · resumable
Stage C

Instant audition

Snapshot cache plays on click; the real instrument loads behind it and takes over at the next note boundary. The rolling eight-bar MIDI buffer, so a preset is auditioned with what you just played, through the part's own chain.

Needs B · the feature people will notice first
Stage D

Versions and diff

Records keep more than one state. The rail, A/B, morph where parameters are exposed, the parameter diff from ParameterModel and the byte diff from PatchDiff. Independent of B — it could go earlier if saving-over is the louder complaint.

Storage policy is the real design work
Stage E

Atlas and substitutes

The map, nearest-neighbour "more like this", and the missing-plug-in substitution flow. All three are the same distance function with three different faces.

Needs B · small once B exists
Stage F

Off the mouse

The browser mirrored to any surface profile that declares a screen: encoders drive the facets, pads hold the top eight, push loads. Generic first; a profile with no screen gets the pads and is told what it does not have.

Reuses the existing surface layer
The boundaries

What this deliberately does not do

  • No cloud, no account, no gallery. The library is files on your disk. A Sound Pack is a portable bundle you can hand somebody; there is no service in the middle, and nothing phones anywhere.
  • No vendor artwork, ever. Plug-in logos and product photographs belong to their makers and this repository is AGPLv3. A sound's tile is drawn from its own audio, which is both legally clean and more informative than a logo — the thumbnail tells you it is a slow pad before you read the name.
  • A substitution never rewrites the record. The rack keeps naming the plug-in it wants, so it plays correctly the day that plug-in comes back. Same rule as ratings surviving a missing source: nothing you did is destroyed by something you did not do.
  • Nothing is tagged behind your back. The analysis proposes; the proposals appear dashed and become real when you accept them. A library that quietly retags itself is a library you stop trusting.
  • It does not scrape a plug-in's own browser. Reading another program's UI is brittle, hostile, and breaks on their next release. Where a plug-in will not expose its presets, the answer is the listen-while-you-work path — and the browser says which instruments are indexed that way, rather than implying it has everything.
  • It is not a sample manager. Audio files are not sounds you play; a browser that mixes the two ends up good at neither.
  • The descriptors are measurements, not opinions. Brightness is a spectral centroid and says so on hover. There is no model here deciding your pad is "emotional".
